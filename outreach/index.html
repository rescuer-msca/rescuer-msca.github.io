Our research, explained

Why do waves grow near the shore? Why do cities flood? Where does pollution go after a storm? Short answers for curious minds, students and teachers.

Try it yourself

A wave tank in your browser

This is a tiny computer model of waves rolling toward a beach. Click or tap anywhere on it to drop a new wave.

  • The white line is the water surface. The dashed line shows the water level when the sea is calm.
  • The sand-colored area is the sea bed: deep on the left, with a sandbar and a sloping beach on the right.
  • Watch the waves on the right: as the water gets shallower they slow down, bunch up and grow taller. That is shoaling.

This toy uses the simplest (linear) wave equations and lets waves fade out near the shore instead of breaking. The models developed in RESCUER also include breaking, wind, currents, moving beds and much more.

Four questions

Water hazards in plain language

Coastal hazards

Why do waves get bigger near the shore?

Out at sea, a wave is energy traveling through the water. When it reaches shallower water, it starts to "feel" the sea bed and slows down. The waves behind keep coming, so the wave gets shorter, and because its energy is squeezed into less water, it grows taller. This is called shoaling. When a wave becomes too steep, it breaks.

The shape of the sea bed matters a lot. Sandbars, reefs and submarine canyons can focus wave energy on one stretch of coast and spare another. That is why forecasting run-up and overtopping at a specific beach or harbor needs models that follow individual waves over a detailed map of the sea floor.

River & estuary dynamics

What happens when a river floods its estuary?

An estuary is where a river meets the sea. Twice a day the tide pushes salty water upstream, while the river pushes fresh water out. After heavy rain, the river carries far more water than usual. If that peak arrives at high tide, or during a storm surge that raises the sea level, the water has nowhere to go and levels rise quickly. Floods caused by several sources at once are called compound floods.

Floods also move the river bed itself: they erode banks, carry sand and gravel, and deposit it elsewhere, which changes how the next flood behaves. Water also seeps into and out of the ground below the river. Good estuary models have to treat all of these processes together.

Urban flooding

Why are city floods so hard to predict?

In a city, streets become rivers. Buildings block the flow, while underpasses, courtyards and curbs steer it in unexpected directions. Drains can fill up and overflow. Parked cars, bins and other debris can be swept along, pile up against bridges and create new obstacles.

Small details make a big difference: the depth and speed of water in one street decide whether people can walk through it safely. Capturing such details means simulating flows with a resolution of about a meter over whole neighborhoods. That takes enormous computing power, which is why we use graphics processors (GPUs). Laboratory experiments with model streets and obstacles check that the simulations are right.

Water quality

Where does pollution go after a storm?

In many cities, rainwater and wastewater share the same pipes. During a heavy storm, the pipes and the treatment plant can be overwhelmed, and part of the untreated mixture overflows into rivers and the sea. With it come bacteria, chemicals and plastic litter.

Once in the sea, pollution does not simply spread evenly. Near the coast, breaking waves stir the water, and currents along and away from the shore carry pollutants to some beaches and out to sea from others. Floating plastic can be washed back and stranded on the sand. Models that combine the sewer network, rivers and waves help authorities decide when a beach is safe for swimming.

Glossary

Words we use

Bathymetry
The shape and depth of the sea or river bed: an underwater map of the terrain.
Boussinesq model
A type of wave model that follows individual waves over varying depth, including how they change shape as they travel. It is much faster than solving the full equations of fluid motion.
Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method
A numerical technique that splits the domain into small elements and describes the solution on each with a polynomial. It reaches high accuracy on complex, irregular meshes.
Estuary
The area where a river meets the sea and fresh water mixes with salt water under the influence of tides.
GPU
Graphics processing unit. Originally made for video games, GPUs perform thousands of calculations at once and can make flood and wave simulations many times faster.
MSCA Doctoral Network
A European Union program under the Marie Skłodowska-Curie Actions that funds international, interdisciplinary PhD training shared by universities, companies and other organizations.
Overtopping
Water from waves passing over the crest of a seawall, dike or breakwater.
Phase-resolving model
A model that computes the motion of every individual wave crest and trough, rather than only average wave properties such as height and period.
Run-up
The highest point on a beach or structure that water reaches as a wave rushes up the slope.
Secondment
A research stay of several weeks or months at another organization in the network, often a company or public authority.
Shallow-water equations
Equations describing flows whose depth is small compared with their horizontal extent, such as rivers, floods and tides. They are the basis of most flood models.
Shoaling
The change in a wave as it moves into shallower water: it slows down, gets shorter and grows taller.
