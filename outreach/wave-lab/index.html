Wave lab

A real research model, running live in your browser. Make waves, watch them break, and follow where pollution goes.

Before you start

A slice through a beach

Both experiments below simulate the same thing: a vertical slice through the sea, from open water to a sandy beach, as if you cut the coast with a knife and looked at it from the side. Waves enter on the left and travel toward the beach on the right.

  1. Offshore

    Away from the coast the water is 5 m deep and the sea bed is flat. Waves travel through it almost unchanged, with rounded crests and long, regular shapes.

  2. Shoaling zone

    Where the bed starts to rise (a slope of 1 m every 20 m), the waves feel the bottom. They slow down, get shorter and grow taller, and their crests become peaked.

  3. Surf zone

    In shallow water the crests become too steep and the waves break. The rest of the way, they travel as foaming fronts (bores) that lose energy to turbulence.

  4. Beach

    At the shoreline each wave runs up the dry slope and back down again (the swash). This is where floating debris ends up and where waves can flood the coast.

How the computer sees it

A computer cannot handle a continuous sea, so the model cuts the slice into small, equal pieces called cells. Each cell stores just two numbers: how deep the water is, on average, and how fast it moves. At every step the model computes how much water and momentum flows through each wall between two cells (a Riemann solver does this), adds the wave-dispersion terms of Nwogu's equations, checks where waves are breaking, and moves forward in time.

The time step comes from the stability rule of the scheme (a Courant number of 0.5): in one step, information may only cross half a cell. Because one step costs less than a millisecond, the model runs faster than real time, which is why it can run live in your browser. Waves are created by a wavemaker inside the water at 45 m, and a sponge layer over the first 35 m absorbs the waves reflected back from the beach, so they do not bounce around the domain. On phones the home-page animation uses half as many cells.

Experiment 1

What is wave breaking?

Far from the coast, waves travel almost unchanged. As they reach shallower water, they slow down, get shorter and grow taller (shoaling). Eventually the crest moves faster than the water in front of it, the wave becomes too steep, and it breaks: its energy turns into turbulence, foam and currents.

A Boussinesq model like BOSZ describes the shape of every single wave, but not the overturning crest itself. So it detects where a wave breaks and adds the turbulence that breaking creates, which drains the wave's energy, just as in the real surf zone. The white foam below marks exactly those places.

Try this

  • Raise the wave height: the waves break further offshore, and the surf zone gets wider.
  • Turn off the breaking treatment: the waves keep their energy all the way to the beach. They grow taller and wobble instead of dissipating.
  • Turn off dispersion: the model becomes the simpler shallow-water equations. Waves turn into steep fronts (bores) much too early and lose their shape. Dispersion is what keeps waves smooth and well shaped.
  • Show water particles: water moves in orbits under a wave, and drifts slowly toward the beach.

Experiment 2

Where does pollution go?

After heavy rain, rivers and sewer overflows can release bacteria and plastic debris near the shore. Whether a beach has to close depends on where that pollution goes, and the breaking waves decide a lot of it.

Two kinds of pollution behave differently. A dissolved pollutant moves with the water and spreads by mixing, which is strongest where waves break. Floating debris rides on the surface, and each passing wave pushes it a little further toward the shore (Stokes drift). That is why plastic piles up on beaches.

This is the subject of RESCUER doctoral project DC10 (water quality).

Try this

  • Release pollutant in the surf zone, then offshore, and compare how fast each spreads.
  • Switch off the turbulent mixing by breaking waves and watch the pollutant stay concentrated.
  • Drop floating debris and count how much of it ends up on the beach.

Under the hood

These experiments run a TypeScript port of the one-dimensional numerics of BOSZ (Boussinesq Ocean & Surf Zone model). BOSZ is developed in the KOSTARISK joint laboratory (Université de Pau et des Pays de l'Adour, SUEZ Rivages Pro Tech and AZTI) and used across RESCUER for nearshore wave modeling.

  • Equations: Nwogu's extended Boussinesq equations, which describe weakly dispersive, fully nonlinear waves from intermediate depth to the shoreline (Nwogu, 1993).
  • Breaking: a breaking criterion (based on the momentum gradient) flags breaking cells. There, a transport equation for the turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) produces an eddy viscosity that dissipates the wave's energy. This kind of turbulence closure for wave breaking in Boussinesq-type models is analyzed in Kazolea & Ricchiuto (2018).
  • Numerics: BOSZ's finite-volume scheme. The water depth and momentum are cell averages; at each cell face a slope-limited reconstruction feeds an HLLC Riemann solver, which captures steep fronts and bores sharply, without spurious oscillations. The dispersive terms use centered differences and a tridiagonal solve, time stepping uses a Runge–Kutta method, and a hydrostatic reconstruction (Audusse et al., 2004) keeps the lake at rest and lets waves run up the dry beach. This follows the hybrid finite-volume approach of the original BOSZ model (Roeber, Cheung & Kobayashi, 2010; Roeber & Cheung, 2012; Mihami & Roeber, 2026).
  • Pollutants: BOSZ's advection–diffusion transport module for dissolved substances and its Lagrangian particle tracking for floating debris. The mixing of dissolved pollutants, which grows with the turbulence of breaking waves (the diffusivity includes the eddy viscosity of the TKE model, and the time step obeys a Péclet-aware stability limit), is being implemented in BOSZ by RESCUER fellow Víctor Loras Herrero (DC10). The Lagrangian particle tracking is a collaboration with Pablo Vallés (UPPA and University of Zaragoza).

Keep in mind that this example is illustrative: it is one-dimensional (a slice through the coast) and depth-averaged, so it leaves out 2D effects such as rip currents, and the undertow that carries some material back offshore.
